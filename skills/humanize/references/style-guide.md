# Humanize style guide

## The editorial stance

Human-sounding writing is not defined by messiness, slang, short sentences, or unusual vocabulary. It is defined by a clear relationship between a writer, a reader, a claim, and the evidence behind it.

The target is:

- specific
- purposeful
- appropriately voiced
- honest about uncertainty
- economical without being skeletal
- natural for the medium

## Voice calibration

When a sample exists, inspect:

- sentence length
- openings
- contractions
- punctuation
- preferred vocabulary
- repeated phrases
- degree of directness
- humor
- certainty
- paragraph length
- use of first person
- tolerance for fragments
- formality

Match those patterns rather than imposing a generic "human" voice.

## Voice profiles

### Casual

Use contractions. Allow first person, fragments, informal transitions, and mild asides where appropriate. Do not manufacture slang.

### Professional

Use clear, direct business prose. Selective contractions are fine. Prefer concrete examples and dry confidence over corporate uplift.

### Technical

Prefer exact terminology. Preserve code-adjacent vocabulary. Use numbers and mechanisms rather than adjectives. Avoid metaphors unless they clarify the mechanism.

### Warm

Allow empathy and shared language. Keep encouragement specific. Avoid cheerleading and empty reassurance.

### Blunt

Use concise sentences and direct claims. Remove pleasantries and redundant caveats. Keep uncertainty when it is real.

## Medium rules

### Blog or article

Start with the actual claim, scene, fact, or tension. Do not open with a generic overview. Use headings only when they improve navigation.

### LinkedIn or social post

Do not force a hook formula, emoji bullets, hashtag stack, or artificial line-by-line rhythm. Let the content create interest.

### Email

State the purpose quickly. Keep greetings and sign-offs when useful. Do not leak markdown or assistant commentary into the message.

### README and documentation

Optimize for accuracy and usability. Do not humanize by making technical prose casual. Describe the current behavior unless the document is about historical change.

### Scientific or academic prose

Keep formal terminology and calibrated uncertainty. Replace vague "research shows" language with named sources when available. Do not add personality merely to lower an AI-style signal.

### Marketing

Persuasion is part of the job. The problem is unsupported hype, not persuasion itself. Prefer proof, features, mechanisms, demonstrations, and verified outcomes.

## Concrete language ladder

When prose is vague, move down this ladder:

1. adjective
2. abstract benefit
3. concrete mechanism
4. observable consequence
5. measured result

Example:

> better performance

becomes, when supported:

> fewer database reads

or:

> p99 latency fell from 900ms to 40ms

Never invent the lower-level detail.

## Voice preservation rules

Keep strong human texture when it is deliberate:

- unusual phrasing
- real sensory detail
- mixed feelings
- specific memories supplied by the author
- self-corrections
- a controlled fragment
- a strong opinion
- a useful aside
- a meaningful repetition

Do not add fake versions of these.

## Rhythm

Do not use sentence-length targets as a checklist.

Instead ask:

- Are several sentences built the same way?
- Are several paragraphs paced identically?
- Does every paragraph end in a punchline?
- Are there too many short fragments in a row?
- Are there too many long sentences carrying multiple unrelated ideas?
- Does the cadence match the author's normal writing?

Fix the actual rhythm problem, not an abstract "burstiness score."

## Rhetoric

Rhetorical devices are allowed.

Keep a contrast when the distinction matters.

Keep a tricolon when the three items are genuinely meaningful.

Keep a rhetorical question when it creates useful tension.

Keep a metaphor when it explains or reveals something rather than merely sounding quotable.

Remove these devices when they are repeated as templates.

## What natural writing should not become

Do not turn anti-slop editing into:

- random sentence lengths
- fake typos
- forced slang
- fake personal anecdotes
- fake uncertainty
- excessive first person
- constant "you"
- short-sentence spam
- deliberate grammatical errors
- anti-formal writing
- detector bait

## Final voice test

Ask:

> Could a reader tell that a particular person wrote this?

Then ask:

> Is that person still the same person who wrote the original?

Both answers should be yes.
